I have been thinking a lot lately about why modern life feels so mentally exhausting even when, um, physically speaking, a lot of us are probably doing less difficult work than people did generations ago. I mean, if you really think about it, many people are not spending their days working in fields, carrying heavy materials, walking enormous distances, or doing, you know, physically dangerous work for twelve hours straight. A lot of us are sitting at desks, looking at screens, answering messages, attending meetings, and moving between relatively comfortable environments. And yet, somehow, people constantly say that they feel drained. They finish the day and they are completely exhausted, even though if you asked them what they physically did, the answer might be, ah, not very much.
And I think part of the reason is that we have become very good at measuring physical effort but not very good at measuring mental switching. That is probably one of the biggest differences. You can sit completely still for six hours and still feel like your brain has run a marathon because during those six hours you might have switched between thirty different contexts. You are writing something, then your phone buzzes, then someone sends you a message, then an email arrives, then you remember something you need to buy, then you return to the document, then somebody asks a question, then you open a new tab, then you see another notification, and, um, before you know it, your attention has been pulled in fifteen directions without you ever really completing one clean stretch of thought.
What is strange is that this kind of exhaustion does not always feel dramatic while it is happening. If you were lifting something heavy, you would know immediately that your muscles were working. You would feel the strain. But mental fragmentation is more subtle. You can be sitting in a chair, drinking coffee, and thinking, well, I am not doing anything particularly difficult. But your brain is constantly loading and unloading different sets of information. It is trying to remember what you were doing before the interruption, what the interruption was about, whether it is urgent, whether you should respond now, whether you need to remember it later, and then, ah, somehow reconstruct the original task afterward.
I think that reconstruction cost is much bigger than people realize. Let us say you are writing a report and you are really focused. You know the argument you are making, you know what paragraph comes next, and you have a kind of mental map of the whole thing. Then somebody messages you and asks a completely unrelated question about next week. You stop, think about next week, look at your calendar, answer the message, maybe open another conversation, and then you come back to the report. The document is still there, obviously, but the mental state you had is gone. You have to rebuild it. You reread the last few lines. You think, ah, where was I going with this? What was the next point? What did I mean by that sentence? That rebuilding might only take a minute or two, but if it happens fifty times a day, the cost becomes enormous.
And then there is the fact that, um, modern life does not really have natural stopping points anymore. That, to me, is one of the strangest things. There used to be more obvious boundaries between activities. You left work and, in many cases, work stayed at work. You went home, and unless there was some emergency, people could not instantly reach you. There was no group chat following you into the evening. There were no work emails arriving while you were eating dinner. There was no little red badge telling you that twelve things were waiting for your attention.
Now, even when you are technically finished with work, there is often a background feeling that you are not actually finished. Your phone contains work, family, friends, news, entertainment, banking, shopping, reminders, appointments, and, ah, basically your entire life in one object. So there is no clear physical separation anymore. You can be lying in bed and suddenly remember something from work because you saw an email notification. You can be at dinner and end up checking a message that changes your mood for the next hour. You can be on vacation and still feel mentally attached to whatever is happening back home because everything is available instantly.

And that constant availability creates a strange kind of low-level tension. Even if nobody is actively demanding anything from you, the possibility is always there. You know somebody could message. You know an email could arrive. You know there could be news. You know there might be something you need to respond to. So, um, part of your attention is always slightly open, almost like a door that never fully closes.

I think this is also why doing nothing has become surprisingly difficult. If you put someone in a room twenty years ago and told them they had to wait ten minutes, they would probably just sit there. Maybe they would look around, think about something, talk to somebody nearby, or just be bored. Now, if there is even a thirty-second pause, most of us instinctively reach for the phone. We are waiting for an elevator, so we check something. We are standing in line, so we check something. We are waiting for food, so we check something. There is almost no empty space left.

And I do not think boredom is necessarily a bad thing. Actually, I think boredom might be one of the places where your brain gets to reorganize itself. You know those moments where you are in the shower or walking somewhere and suddenly you remember something important or come up with a good idea? That often happens when the brain is not being fed constant input. But now, um, we fill almost every gap with information. We are listening to podcasts while walking. We are watching videos while eating. We are checking social media while waiting. We are scrolling while watching television. Sometimes we are consuming two things at the same time, which is, ah, kind of ridiculous when you think about it.

There is also a difference between being informed and being overloaded. I think we often confuse those two things. We assume that more information must be better because information is useful. And obviously information can be useful. But if you are consuming hundreds of pieces of information every day, most of which you cannot act on, then eventually it starts becoming noise.

Imagine how many things the average person now knows about that have absolutely no direct relevance to their day. You might wake up and, within twenty minutes, know about a political argument in another country, a celebrity controversy, a company announcement, a natural disaster thousands of miles away, a sports result, somebody else's vacation, a stranger's opinion about a movie, and, um, five different advertisements telling you that you should buy something. Your brain has to process all of that even though almost none of it requires a real decision from you.

And that matters because emotionally, the brain does not always treat distant events as distant. If you see something upsetting, you can feel upset even if it has no immediate connection to your physical environment. So you can be sitting safely in your kitchen drinking coffee while your nervous system is reacting to ten separate crises that you saw on a screen. That is a very unusual situation historically. For most of human history, the problems people knew about were largely the problems happening around them. Now you can carry the emotional weight of the entire planet in your pocket.

I am not saying that people should ignore the world. That would be, um, obviously absurd. But I do think there is a difference between understanding what is happening and continuously exposing yourself to every update. News platforms, social media platforms, and apps generally do not have an incentive to tell you, okay, you understand enough now, please leave. Their incentive is usually the opposite. They want you to stay. So information is presented as an endless stream.

There is always one more thing. One more article. One more clip. One more reaction. One more update. And the problem with an endless stream is that your brain never gets the feeling of completion. You cannot finish social media. You cannot reach the end of the internet. There is no moment where the screen says, congratulations, you have now consumed enough information for today.

That lack of completion is, ah, psychologically interesting because human beings seem to like finishing things. There is satisfaction in completing a book, finishing a meal, cleaning a room, or reaching the end of a task. But many digital experiences are specifically designed not to end. You scroll and scroll and scroll. The next thing appears automatically. There is no natural stopping cue.

And so you end up in this strange state where you have spent forty minutes doing something without ever deciding to do it. That is probably one of the most frustrating feelings. You open your phone to check one message and then, somehow, twenty-five minutes disappear. You were not even enjoying yourself that much. You were just, um, pulled along by whatever appeared next.

Then afterward you feel annoyed because you lost time, and that creates another layer of mental fatigue. Now you are not only tired from consuming all that information, but you are also slightly angry at yourself for wasting time. So you think, right, I need to be productive now. And then you sit down to work while already feeling mentally scattered.

This is where the productivity industry becomes interesting because, ah, a lot of the advice is basically about managing symptoms created by an environment that constantly fragments attention. We download focus apps because other apps are distracting us. We buy planners because our schedules are overloaded. We watch videos about how to stop watching videos. There is something almost funny about that.

I have definitely done this myself. I have spent, you know, forty minutes watching someone explain the perfect productivity system instead of doing the thing I was supposed to do. And while I am watching it, I feel productive because the content is about productivity. That is a very convenient loophole. You can procrastinate while feeling responsible.

Another part of modern mental exhaustion is the number of small decisions we make every day. People usually think of decision-making in terms of major choices, like where to live or what job to take. But most decisions are tiny. What should I eat? Should I answer this now? Should I buy this? Which video should I watch? Which route should I take? Should I go out tonight? Which message should I respond to first? What should I wear? Should I click this notification? Should I save this article?

Each decision is small, but there are thousands of them. And, um, technology has increased the number of available options in almost every area of life. You used to have a few television channels. Now you can open a streaming service and spend twenty minutes deciding what to watch because there are ten thousand options. You used to go to a local store and choose between a few products. Now you can compare hundreds of versions of the same thing online.

More choice sounds like freedom, and in many ways it is. But it also means more comparison, more uncertainty, and more opportunities to wonder whether you chose incorrectly. You order something and then you see another option and think, ah, maybe I should have bought that one. You choose a movie and spend the first ten minutes wondering if another movie would have been better. You pick a restaurant and then check reviews while you are already there.

That constant comparison can make perfectly good experiences feel less satisfying. Instead of asking, do I enjoy this, we start asking, is this the best possible option? And the problem is that there is almost always another option somewhere. So satisfaction becomes difficult because the comparison never ends.

Social media makes that even more intense because now you are not only comparing products or entertainment, you are comparing lives. And, um, logically, everybody knows that people usually post highlights. Nobody uploads a beautifully edited montage of themselves sitting in traffic, paying bills, arguing over something stupid, or staring at the refrigerator trying to decide what to eat. People post trips, achievements, celebrations, new jobs, new houses, relationships, and whatever else looks interesting.

But even when you know that intellectually, it can still affect you emotionally. You look at twenty people having their best moments and your brain compares that collection of highlights to your ordinary Tuesday. Of course your Tuesday looks boring. It is competing against twenty different people's vacations, promotions, weddings, and achievements at once.

And I think that creates this subtle pressure that your life should constantly be interesting. You should be improving, traveling, learning, exercising, building something, making money, socializing, and, ah, somehow also resting properly. There is a very strange modern expectation that you should optimize every part of yourself.

You should have an ideal morning routine. You should eat perfectly. You should sleep perfectly. You should exercise efficiently. You should read. You should meditate. You should maintain friendships. You should advance your career. You should manage your finances. You should have hobbies. You should keep your home organized. You should stay informed. You should travel. You should build a personal brand. And then, somehow, you are also supposed to relax.

Relaxation itself becomes another task. People say, okay, I need to improve my sleep score. I need to meditate for exactly fifteen minutes. I need to get ten thousand steps. I need to optimize my recovery. And, um, these things can be useful, but there is a point where self-improvement becomes another source of pressure.

There is something exhausting about constantly treating yourself as a project that is not finished yet. You wake up and immediately have data telling you how well you slept, whether you recovered properly, how many steps you took yesterday, how productive you were, how much money you spent, and how many messages you have not answered. Before breakfast, you already have a performance review.

Sometimes I think it would be healthier to have areas of life where there is no score. You go for a walk because you want to walk, not because you need to close a ring. You read because the book is interesting, not because you need to hit a yearly reading target. You see a friend because you enjoy their company, not because maintaining social relationships is on some wellness checklist.

I am not saying measurement is bad. Measurement can be incredibly useful. But once you measure something, it can become difficult not to optimize it. And once you start optimizing it, um, it can stop feeling natural.

Another thing that contributes to mental exhaustion is that modern work is often ambiguous. Physical work tends to have clearer outputs. If your job is to build ten chairs, you know when ten chairs are built. But knowledge work can be endless. You can always improve the presentation. You can always send another email. You can always analyze more data. You can always refine the document.

So there is often no objective moment where the work is done. You just stop because the day ended or because the deadline arrived. That means part of your brain can continue thinking about the task afterward. You wonder whether you should have changed something. You remember an email you forgot. You think about tomorrow's meeting.

There is actually a term people sometimes use for unfinished tasks staying active in your mind, and regardless of the exact psychology behind it, the experience is familiar. Unfinished things have a way of following you around. A task you have not completed can feel heavier than a task that took longer but is finished.

That is why, um, sometimes writing things down helps so much. It is not necessarily because the list magically gives you more time. It just gives your brain permission to stop holding everything internally. If you know the task exists somewhere reliable, you do not have to keep mentally repeating it.

I think a lot of modern anxiety is basically an open-tabs problem. Not literally browser tabs, although those too, but mental tabs. You have twenty things half-open. Something you need to buy. Someone you need to call. A message you need to answer. A decision you have not made. A document you need to finish. An appointment you need to schedule. A subscription you need to cancel.

None of them individually is catastrophic, but together they create this constant background noise. You are trying to relax, but somewhere in your head there is a list running quietly.

And that is probably why people sometimes feel unbelievably relieved after doing something that took five minutes. You finally make the phone call you have been avoiding for three weeks, and suddenly you feel lighter. The task was not difficult. Carrying the task mentally for three weeks was the exhausting part.

I also think our relationship with speed has changed. Almost everything is expected to happen faster now. Messages are instant, deliveries are fast, information is immediate, and entertainment starts on demand. So our tolerance for waiting has decreased.

If a webpage takes five seconds to load, it feels broken. If somebody does not answer a message for several hours, we sometimes wonder what happened. If a package takes a week to arrive, it feels slow. And, ah, once you get used to immediate responses, normal human processes can start feeling frustrating because many important things are still slow.

Learning something deeply is slow. Building trust is slow. Getting good at a skill is slow. Improving your health is slow. Building a career is slow. Saving money is slow. Creating something meaningful is usually slow.

But our brains are surrounded by systems that give instant feedback. You post something and immediately get reactions. You search something and immediately get answers. You order food and immediately see where the driver is. So when you work on something that takes six months to show progress, it can feel unusually difficult.

There is also this strange pressure to respond quickly. If somebody sends a message, you can see it instantly, so there is a social expectation that you should answer relatively soon. And if you have multiple communication platforms, that becomes a lot. Email, text messages, work chat, social media messages, group chats, notifications.

Each platform creates its own little queue of obligations.

And this is where I think boundaries matter more than productivity tricks. Sometimes the solution is not figuring out how to process messages faster. Sometimes the solution is deciding that you do not need to be continuously available.

That sounds obvious, but in practice it can feel uncomfortable because you worry that somebody might think you are ignoring them. So you train people, unintentionally, to expect immediate responses. If you always answer within two minutes, then twenty minutes starts looking slow.

I think there is value in making communication slightly less immediate. Not ignoring important things, obviously, but allowing normal delays. There is something psychologically healthy about remembering that a message is not necessarily a command.

The same applies to notifications. A notification is basically an external system saying, look at me now. And, um, once you realize that, it becomes strange how many systems are allowed to interrupt you whenever they want. A shopping app can interrupt your conversation because shoes are on sale. A social platform can interrupt your work because someone liked a photo. A game can interrupt dinner because some virtual event started.

If an actual person walked into your room every five minutes to tell you those things, you would probably ask them to stop. But because it happens silently through a screen, we accept it.

And I think one of the simplest things people can do for their attention is reduce the number of things that are allowed to demand it. You do not necessarily need stronger willpower. You can change the environment.

That is something I wish productivity conversations focused on more. People talk about discipline as if your job is to resist temptation constantly. But resisting temptation all day is exhausting. It is often easier to make the temptation less available.

If you are trying to focus and your phone is beside you, you are using some amount of mental energy not to check it. Even if you do not touch it, part of your brain knows it is there. If you put it in another room, the decision disappears.

That is a small example, but the principle is powerful. Good environments reduce the number of decisions you need to make.

And maybe that is the larger point. A lot of mental exhaustion comes not from doing one incredibly difficult thing, but from doing hundreds of tiny things badly connected together. It comes from interruptions, unfinished tasks, constant information, endless choices, comparison, and the feeling that you should always be doing something else.

You can be working and feel guilty that you are not exercising. You can be exercising and feel guilty that you are not working. You can be resting and feel guilty that you are not being productive. You can be with friends and wonder whether you should be answering messages. You can be on vacation and think about what is happening at work.

So you are physically in one place but mentally divided between several places.

And, ah, I think the opposite of that is not necessarily relaxation. The opposite might be presence. Just doing one thing without simultaneously measuring yourself against five other things.

If you are working, work. If you are eating, eat. If you are talking to someone, talk to them. If you are resting, actually rest.

That sounds almost embarrassingly simple, but it has become surprisingly difficult.

I think there is a reason people describe activities like hiking, cooking, gardening, painting, or working with their hands as calming. Those activities often force your attention into one place. You cannot really chop vegetables while simultaneously answering three emails. You cannot easily climb a trail while switching between six browser tabs.

Your mind gets a temporary break from context switching.

And that might be what people are actually seeking when they say they want to disconnect. They do not necessarily want zero technology forever. They want fewer competing demands on their attention.

Because attention is probably one of the most valuable things we have. Your experience of life is basically whatever you pay attention to. If your attention is constantly fragmented, then your experience becomes fragmented too.

And that is what worries me slightly about the direction things are going. We keep building systems that are better and better at capturing attention, but we are not necessarily getting better at deciding where our attention should go.

The technology is improving faster than our habits.

And, um, I do not think the solution is some dramatic rejection of modern life. I am not going to throw my phone into a lake and move into a cabin. I like technology. It is incredibly useful. I like being able to talk to people instantly, look things up, navigate anywhere, listen to music, and do a hundred things from one device.

But I do think using powerful tools well requires some deliberate limits.

Maybe you do not need notifications from everything. Maybe you do not need to read news every hour. Maybe every message does not require an immediate answer. Maybe you do not need your phone beside you while you work. Maybe you do not need entertainment filling every moment of boredom.

And maybe, um, the feeling of being behind is partly an illusion created by constantly seeing what everybody else is doing.

Because there will always be someone doing more. Someone earning more. Someone traveling more. Someone reading more. Someone exercising more. Someone building something impressive.

If your standard is the entire internet, you will always be losing.

But your life is not actually happening on the entire internet. It is happening in the small space around you. It is happening in the conversation you are having, the work you are doing, the people you care about, and the few things that genuinely matter to you.

I think simplifying attention is probably more realistic than simplifying life. Life will always be complicated. There will always be responsibilities, problems, unexpected events, and difficult periods. But maybe you can decide how many of those things deserve access to your mind at the same moment.

You do not need to solve tomorrow while eating dinner tonight.

You do not need to carry every unfinished task in your head.

You do not need to know everything happening everywhere.

And you probably do not need to become the perfectly optimized version of yourself.

Maybe being mentally rested is not about doing nothing. Maybe it is about doing fewer things at once.

Because when I think about the days where I feel best, it is usually not because nothing happened. Sometimes those days are actually very busy. The difference is that the day had some coherence. I did one thing, then another thing, then another thing. I was not being constantly dragged between them.

And that, um, might be one of the simplest definitions of a good day now. Not necessarily a day where you accomplished everything, but a day where your attention mostly belonged to you.
